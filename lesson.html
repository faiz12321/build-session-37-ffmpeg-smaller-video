Common mistakes

  1. Trusting stream presence as proof of good sound. A named audio codec can still carry silence or unwanted content. The fixture helper decodes samples and checks a nonzero tone; it does not understand speech or music.
  2. Assuming CRF guarantees a target size. Higher CRF generally reduces video quality and bitrate, but source content and audio settings affect the whole file. Measure the actual result.
  3. Reusing an old output without -n. Use -n on each ffmpeg write and keep the input. An ordinary overwrite prompt is not a workflow to rely on.
  4. Assuming one decoded fixture proves phone compatibility. Watch and listen on the actual receiving device. This lesson does not test that.

Frequently asked questions

What does -n do?

It tells ffmpeg not to overwrite an existing output and to exit instead. These exercises check existing nonempty and empty output files remain unchanged. This is not a proof against concurrent filesystem races.

Does AAC in ffprobe mean the sound is good?

No. It identifies a stream. The helper also decodes video and audio and checks enough audio samples and a nonzero RMS value for this known tone. It cannot judge meaning, sync or quality for arbitrary footage.

Will every video get smaller?

No. CRF is a quality control, not a file-size target. The recorded generated fixture got smaller; other sources may not. Compare actual size and quality.

Is extracted audio re-encoded?

-c:a copy copies the compressed audio stream into another container without a new lossy audio encode. Container headers and file bytes can differ; it is not a byte-identical copy of the whole source.

Is this a full backup or device-compatibility check?

No. The input digest checks source bytes for this run, and fixture checks cover specific properties. They do not cover all metadata, tracks, subtitles, visual quality or phone playback.
